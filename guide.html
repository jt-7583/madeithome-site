1. The big idea

Made It Home exists to do one thing well: let a parent know their kid got home, so everyone can relax. On a normal night the app is silent and knows almost nothing — just whether a check-in happened by the time you set. It only speaks up when it doesn't.

It is deliberately not a live tracker. There's no map following your kid around, no constant location history. The app only ever answers one question: home, or not yet? That restraint is the whole point — it's the thing that makes kids willing to use it.

2. Parent & kid: one app, two roles

There's a single app. The first time it opens, each phone chooses a role:

  • A parent's phone — sets curfews, sees status, and gets the alerts.
  • A kid's phone — shows the Check In button and the SOS button.

The choice is sticky on purpose: switching means reinstalling. That prevents a kid from quietly flipping their own phone into "parent" mode.

3. Den codes — your family key

A den is your private family space. When the first parent sets it up, Made It Home creates a unique code for you — you don't pick it, so no two families can ever end up with the same one. Everyone else in your family — the other parent, each kid — joins by entering that code.

Why the code matters: it's the key that ties every phone to your family and no one else's. When someone enters your code, the app shows them your family's name to confirm before they join — so a mistyped code can't land a kid in the wrong family. Alerts only ever travel within a single den, and someone who installs the app without your code can never see your kids.

Share your den code only with your own family — treat it like a house key.

4. Setup, start to finish

Step 1 — First parent sets up. Install the app, choose "A parent's," tap Start a new den, and name your family (e.g. "The Cotton family"). The app gives you a unique den code to share. Add your name (e.g. "Mom"). You've just created your family's den.
Step 2 — Add the kid's phone. On your child's phone, install the app, choose "A kid's," enter their name and your den code. The app shows "Is this your family?" with your family's name — they tap Yes, join and they're in your den.
Step 3 — Approve home Wi-Fi. While the kid is home and on your home network, their app asks to use it as home. You approve it from your phone (see the Wi-Fi section below for why this step exists).
Step 4 — Set tonight's curfew. On your phone, pick a be-home-by time. Optionally set a private grace period. Done.
Step 5 — Live your life. When your kid gets home, they tap Check In. If they don't by the deadline (plus grace), your phone rings.

5. Wi-Fi: how it works & why it keeps kids safe

Why Wi-Fi instead of GPS?

Most family apps use GPS, which is accurate to maybe tens of meters — enough to be wrong about which building someone's in, especially indoors or in apartments. Being joined to your specific home Wi-Fi network is a much stronger signal that the phone is truly inside your home, not just nearby. So Made It Home confirms arrival by the phone being on your home network, and uses that to decide whether to even show the Check In button.

Why do parents approve the network?

The Check In button only appears when the kid's phone is on a network you've approved. That approval is the security boundary. A kid can't check in "from home" while they're actually somewhere else, because a random coffee-shop or friend's network isn't on your approved list. And they can't sneak a new network onto the list themselves — the kid's phone can only propose a network; a parent has to approve it.

Nice try, renamed hotspot. Even if someone renames a phone hotspot to match your home network's name, it's still a request that lands in your approval queue — you decide what counts as home.

Traveling? Temporary home base.

On a trip, the kid can ask to use the hotel or grandma's Wi-Fi as a temporary home base. You approve it, and it expires on its own when the trip ends — it never permanently replaces your real home network, and you don't have to remember to remove it.

6. A night, step by step

  1. You set a curfew — say 10:30 PM. Your kid sees the deadline; they never see the grace period, so the deadline keeps its meaning.
  2. They head out. The app does nothing while they're gone. No tracking.
  3. They get home and tap Check In. The button only appears on your approved Wi-Fi, and the tap is confirmed with Face ID — so it's really them, not a friend holding the phone.
  4. You see "made it home." A warm confirmation on your phone. That's the whole job on a good night.
  5. If the deadline passes with no check-in — after your grace period — your phone rings like a real call, even on silent, and keeps ringing until the kid checks in or you answer. You also see their last known location as a starting point.
Grace period is your private buffer between the deadline and the alert — for the kid who's reliably five minutes late. Only you see it; to your kid, the deadline is the deadline.

7. The SOS button

Any time your kid feels unsafe, they can tap "I need help." It immediately alerts every parent in your den and shares their location — no waiting for a deadline. It's the one moment the app shares location on the kid's own initiative. Answering or resolving it on your end stops the alert.

Honest about limits: Made It Home alerts you, the parent — it is not a 911 service and doesn't dispatch emergency responders. It depends on the phone having signal and battery. It's the fastest way to reach your people, not a replacement for calling for help directly.

8. Safety timers — when your kid arms the net

Made It Home isn't only for parents to set. Before a walk home, a party, a shift, or a first date, your kid can arm their own safety timer — a check-in they set for themselves. There are two kinds, and the difference is who knows about it.

"Tell my family." They set a check-in and you're told now. If they don't check in by the deadline, you're alerted — exactly like a curfew, but on their initiative. Good for "I'll be home by 11."
"Keep it private." They set a quiet timer and write a short note — for example, "Out with Bob S. from class, back by 11." You are not told it exists. If they check in by the deadline, it stays private forever — you never see it.
The private timer is the point: if it runs out with no check-in, it becomes an SOS — sending you their note and last known location, at the exact moment it matters. A few minutes before the deadline we quietly remind them to check in, so a busy-but-safe kid doesn't set off a false alarm.

It's independence for them with a safety net underneath: they stay in control, and you're only pulled in if something actually goes wrong. The "I'm safe" tap works from anywhere — being safe isn't the same as being home.

9. Two parents, one house

The first parent creates the den (Step 1 above). The second parent installs the app, chooses "A parent's," taps Join with a code, and enters the same den code — confirming the family name. Each adds their own name. Now:

  • Both of you see the same kids and the same status.
  • When you set a curfew, you choose whether the alert rings both parents or just you.
  • Either parent can approve a network request or respond to an alert.

10. Two parents, two houses

Made It Home is built for split households too. One parent creates the den and shares the code; the other parent taps Join with a code to join the same den, and the one kid is linked to both of you. The key detail is Wi-Fi:

  • The kid approves each home's Wi-Fi — Mom's network and Dad's network both end up on their approved list. Checking in works at either house.
  • Whoever sets a given night's curfew chooses who gets alerted — both parents, or just the one whose night it is.
  • For a trip from either house, the temporary travel-network works the same way and expires on its own.
Cooperative by design: both parents share one den code, so this works best when co-parents are willing to share that key. Everyone linked to the kid can see their status — because anyone setting a curfew has a genuine reason to know their kid got home safe.

11. What we do & don't collect

  • We collect: the kid's name, the current Wi-Fi network name (to confirm arrival), check-in times, and a sparse location that is only revealed to a parent during a missed check-in or an SOS.
  • We don't: track continuously, show a live map, sell data, or run ads. On an on-time night, no one ever sees where your kid has been.
  • Private safety timers stay private: if your kid arms a private timer and checks in on time, you're never told it existed, and the note they wrote is discarded. It only ever reaches you if they miss it.

12. Common questions

What if my kid's phone dies or loses signal?

The app can't confirm a check-in it never receives. A dead or offline phone looks like "not yet checked in," which is exactly when you'd want to follow up anyway.

Does the alert really wake me on silent?

It rings like a phone call and keeps re-ringing. (We're also adding Apple's Critical Alerts, which break through silent mode and Do Not Disturb even more forcefully.)

Can my kid set their own check-in, without me?

Yes — that's the safety timer (section 8). They can either tell you they've set one, or keep it private. A private timer only ever reaches you if they miss it, at which point it becomes an SOS with their note and location. If they check in on time, you never see it. It's designed so an older kid gets a safety net without feeling watched.

Can my kid fake being home?

The Check In button only appears on a parent-approved network, and the tap requires Face ID. Faking it would take approving a fraudulent network — which only a parent can do.

Could my kid end up in the wrong family's den?

No. You don't pick your code — the app generates a unique one for you, so two families can't share a code. And when anyone enters a code to join, the app shows them your family's name to confirm first, so a typo can't quietly put a kid in a stranger's den. (Full account-based sign-in is on the way to make this even stronger.)
